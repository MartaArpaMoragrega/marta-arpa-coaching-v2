Giving improvement feedback is one of those things almost everyone finds hard. I prefer to call it that because its purpose is to help someone improve, not to point out what is wrong. Sometimes we avoid it until the problem has grown. Other times we soften it so much that the other person doesn't get the message at all. And other times we let it all out at once, along with the tension we have been holding.

There is no magic formula, but there are a few ideas I have seen work while accompanying people who manage teams. Maybe they will help you, and I'm sure you have your own.

### 1. Before you speak, ask yourself what it's for

Before the conversation, it helps to be clear about what you want to achieve: for something to change? For the other person to understand the impact? To vent your frustration? The first two help. The third usually ends badly.

If you notice you are very angry, it is sometimes better to wait a day. Not to avoid it, but to arrive with a calmer head.

### 2. Speak early and in private

The more time passes, the heavier it gets. A timely comment about something small is usually much easier than a huge conversation three months later.

And, whenever possible, somewhere quiet and without an audience. A correction in front of others feels like humiliation, even if that is not your intention.

### 3. Start with facts, not judgement

A judgement sounds like an attack: "you're always late", "you don't care". A fact is something you can look at together: "on Tuesday you arrived twenty minutes late to the client meeting".

Describing what you have seen, without labelling the person, gives them a real chance to respond without getting defensive.

### 4. Explain the impact

Often the person doesn't know what effect their actions have. Explaining it calmly helps them understand: "when that happens, the client waits and the team has to improvise".

There is no need to dramatise. Being concrete and honest is enough.

### 5. Listen to their side

Sometimes there is information you don't have. There may be a reason, a misunderstanding or something they are struggling with that you hadn't noticed. Asking with genuine curiosity ("how do you see it?") changes the tone of the conversation.

Listening doesn't mean agreeing with everything, but it does give you a fuller picture before deciding what to do.

### 6. Agree on what changes

A feedback conversation that ends without any commitment fades away. Ask what the person proposes, add what you need, and agree on something small and concrete, with a date to review it.

### A simple framework to remember

1. **Fact:** what happened, as you saw it.
2. **Impact:** what effect it has on the team, the client or the work.
3. **Question:** how the other person sees it.
4. **Agreement:** what will be done differently and when you will review it.

### Some common traps

- **Piling up** small details until they explode in a single conversation.
- **Wrapping it up** in so much praise that the real message gets lost.
- **Doing it in writing** when the subject is delicate and is better discussed face to face.
- **Generalising** with "always" and "never", which are almost never true and put the person on the defensive.

### To finish

Giving improvement feedback doesn't become easy, but with practice it becomes more natural, and I believe it is a form of respect: it means I care enough to tell you.
