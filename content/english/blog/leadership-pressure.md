If you manage people, projects or resources, pressure is part of the job. It comes from above, from the sides and sometimes from yourself. And it doesn't go away, however hard we try.

This year's [Gallup report](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/349484/state-workplace.aspx) suggests that people who lead say they feel more stress and more loneliness than those who don't manage anyone. That doesn't surprise me, and I don't think it's a personal failing either.

No pressure gets solved in one go. But there are small practices that tend to work. They aren't recipes, just ideas to try out, keeping whatever works for you.

### 1. Tell apart where the pressure comes from

Not all pressure is the same. Some of it comes from outside: deadlines, targets, requests. Some of it we put on ourselves: demands nobody has asked of us, the wish for everything to be perfect, the fear of letting people down.

Naming it helps. With a notebook and two columns, "put on me" and "I put on myself", you often see something that was mixed up before.

### 2. Decide what you won't do this week

Every to-do list talks about what needs doing. Almost none talks about what won't get done, and that is what brings the most relief.

Pick one or two things you can postpone, delegate or stop doing, and say it out loud, to your team or your manager. What goes unsaid tends to keep weighing on you.

### 3. Protect some time to think

When your calendar is full of meetings, thinking gets pushed to the evening or the weekend. A short block in the calendar, even just thirty minutes, treated with the same respect as a client meeting, can change the rhythm of your week quite a lot.

### 4. Take small breaks between one thing and the next

Going from a difficult meeting to the next one without a breather means we carry the stress from one to the other. Two or three minutes to stand up, drink some water or look out of the window help you start the next conversation with a clearer head.

### 5. Talk to someone outside your team

Leading can be lonely. The people who report to you aren't always the best ones to tell what's worrying you, and neither is your manager. A colleague from another area, a mentor, someone you trust or a coach can offer a good space to think out loud.

### 6. Say what you need to those above you

We often assume our manager already knows what we're struggling with. It's almost never the case. Asking for something specific, "I need us to prioritise these two goals" or "I need more clarity on this", is usually better received than we imagine.

### 7. At the end of the day, notice one thing that went well

Under a lot of pressure, the mind holds on to what's missing. Writing down one thing that went well, however small, tips the balance a little. It doesn't change the situation, but it does change how you look at it.

### To finish

You don't need to follow all seven. Try just one this week and you're already looking after yourself, and that is also looking after your team.
